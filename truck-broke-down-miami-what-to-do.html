Truck Broke Down on a Miami Highway? Here's Exactly What to Do

Stay safe, protect your load, and get rolling again — a step-by-step guide for I-95, I-75, the Palmetto and the Turnpike.

Breaking down in a loaded rig is stressful anywhere — but doing it in South Florida traffic, maybe far from your home shop, is worse. The good news: if you handle the first few minutes right, you stay safe and you get moving again a lot faster. Here's the exact playbook we give drivers who call us off I-95, I-75, the Palmetto (826) and Florida's Turnpike.

1. Get off the road — safely

The moment you feel a problem — a warning light, a loss of power, a blowout, smoke — put on your hazards and start working toward the right shoulder or an exit. On Miami's busiest stretches, the left shoulder is a dangerous place to sit; aim for the right shoulder, a ramp, or a gas station or truck stop if you can still roll.

Tip: If you can safely reach the next exit, do it. A truck stopped on an off-ramp or a lot is far safer — and easier for a mechanic to reach — than one on a live shoulder in fast traffic.

2. Make yourself visible

Once stopped, protect your space:

  • Leave your hazard lights on.
  • Set out your three reflective warning triangles — one about 10 feet behind the truck, one around 100 feet back, and one around 200 feet back (further on a fast highway or a curve).
  • Stay out of the traffic side of the truck. Exit and wait on the shoulder side, away from moving lanes.

3. Do a quick, safe assessment

You don't need to be a mechanic — you just need to tell someone what happened. Note the basics:

  • What warning lights are on? (check engine, DPF, low oil, temperature, ABS)
  • Any noise, smoke, smell, or a leak under the truck?
  • Did it lose power, overheat, or just not start?
  • Is it a tire — steer, drive or trailer?

That 20-second summary is what lets a mechanic show up with the right tools and parts the first time.

4. Call a mobile diesel mechanic — not just a tow

A lot of highway breakdowns are things that can be fixed right where you sit: a sensor, a hose or belt, a fuel or air problem, a dead battery, a regen issue, or a flat. Towing a loaded truck to a shop is expensive and slow, and you still wait in line when you get there. A mobile diesel mechanic comes to you, diagnoses on the spot, and often gets you rolling the same visit.

If it turns out to be a major failure, then a tow is the right call — and we can arrange heavy-duty towing too. The point is to find out which one you actually need before paying for a tow you might not.

When you call, have ready: your highway and direction (e.g., "I-95 south"), the nearest exit or mile marker, your truck's make and engine, and what happened. Clear location + clear symptom = faster help.

Where breakdowns happen most around Miami

We get the most calls from a few predictable places — heavy traffic, merges and long grades wear trucks down:

  • I-95 through Miami-Dade and Broward — stop-and-go traffic and hot running.
  • I-75 and Alligator Alley — long, remote stretches heading west.
  • Florida's Turnpike — high-speed miles with few places to stop.
  • The Palmetto (826) and Dolphin (836) interchanges — constant merging near Doral and Medley.
  • Near the ports — Port of Miami and Port Everglades, where drayage trucks run all day.
